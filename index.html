Example: a SaaS startup from founding to Series B

Company at founding

Shares held by each founder before any outside money.

Funding rounds

Amounts in ₹ crore. The pool target is the ESOP pool as a % of post-money, topped up before the round (the option pool shuffle).

Pricing a round

Pre-money valuation is what the company is worth before the new money. Post-money adds the investment. The price per share is set on the fully diluted share count before the round, including any new pool shares.

Post-money = Pre-money + Investment Price per share = Pre-money ÷ (Shares before round + New pool shares) Investor shares = Investment ÷ Price per share Investor stake = Investment ÷ Post-money

The option pool shuffle

Investors usually ask for the ESOP pool to be a set % of the company after the round, and for it to be created before they invest. The new pool shares therefore dilute only the existing shareholders, not the incoming investor, which quietly lowers the effective pre-money value for founders.

New pool shares X solves: Existing pool + X = Target % × Post-round shares X = (Target % × S × k − Existing pool) ÷ (1 − Target % × k), k = Post-money ÷ Pre-money

The “effective pre-money” in Round economics shows the valuation founders actually received once the pool top-up is taken out.

Liquidation preference

At exit, investors with a 1x preference can take their money back before common shareholders get anything. A non-participating investor chooses the better of the preference or converting to common and sharing pro rata. A participating investor takes the preference and then also shares pro rata, which is why participation is negotiated hard.

Non-participating payout = max(Preference × Invested, Stake × Proceeds left for common) Participating payout = Preference × Invested + Stake × Proceeds left for common

With stacked seniority, the latest round is paid first; with pari passu, all preferences share any shortfall in proportion to their claims. The simulator works out each investor’s convert-or-take-preference choice together, because one investor converting changes what the others receive.

Assumptions

The whole ESOP pool is treated as granted and exercised at exit, which slightly overstates employee payouts. Shares are not rounded to whole numbers. There are no convertible notes, SAFEs, anti-dilution adjustments, secondary sales or participation caps. Taxes and transaction costs are ignored.

The three ideas behind every cap table

Illustrated with the Series A round from the example: ₹15 cr invested at a ₹60 cr pre-money valuation.

Pre-money and post-money

The investor’s stake is the investment divided by the post-money value: 15 ÷ 75 = 20%. Everyone else shares the remaining 80%.

The option pool shuffle

Investors ask for a bigger ESOP pool created before they invest, so the new shares dilute only existing holders. The headline valuation stays the same while the founders’ real price drops.

Liquidation preference

In a modest sale, investors take their money back first, latest round first. What is left goes to founders, employees and anyone who chose to convert. In a large sale, everyone converts and ownership decides.

Fully diluted shares

All shares that exist or could exist: issued shares plus the full ESOP pool, as if every option were granted and exercised. Ownership in this simulator is always shown on this basis.

Step-up

The price per share in a round divided by the price in the previous round. A 3x step-up means new investors paid three times what the last round paid for each share.

Participating vs non-participating preference

A non-participating investor takes either the preference or a pro rata share of the proceeds, whichever is larger. A participating investor gets the preference and then also shares in what is left, which is better for the investor and worse for founders.

Stacked vs pari passu seniority

Stacked means later rounds are paid before earlier ones. Pari passu means all preferred investors stand in one line and share any shortfall in proportion to what they are owed.

Preference overhang

The gap between what founders own and what they receive at a low exit. It shrinks as the exit value grows past the point where investors prefer to convert.
